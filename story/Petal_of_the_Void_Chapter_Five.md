# PETAL OF THE VOID

## Chapter Five: The Shape of Ordinary Things

---

The written examination was held in a long stone hall near the imperial district. The ceiling was vaulted, high enough that the candles along the walls left the upper third of the room in darkness. Five hundred candidates sat in rows at low writing desks, each with a brush, an inkstone, and a paper bearing forty questions. The presiding official announced the time limit: two hours.

She read the first question. *Describe the five primary elemental alignments and their theoretical relationship to meridian pathway development.* She had read the answer to this in Silverwind City's restricted section, in the Capital's central archive, and in three additional texts she had found in the Capital's second library. She wrote the answer.

She read the second question. Wrote the answer.

She read the third, fourth, fifth. Wrote each answer. Every character of every relevant text was available in her memory with perfect fidelity, no degradation, no uncertainty. The brush moved across the paper in clean strokes.

She finished the fortieth question, set down her brush, and looked at the clock on the wall. Eleven minutes had passed.

She placed the brush in its holder and folded her hands in her lap.

Three seats to her left, Chen Mao was working with the focused intensity of someone who had prepared and intended to prove it. His brush moved steadily, paused, moved again. She had observed him studying for the past three evenings at the inn, muttering sect histories under his breath while she sat at the same table reading. He had a good memory for things he cared about. He cared about this.

She sat for one hour and forty-nine minutes with her hands folded, reviewing her answers with a fraction of her processing attention while the rest catalogued the room. The candidate two rows ahead of her was stuck on question twenty-three (she could tell from the angle of his head and the duration of his pause). The candidate directly behind her had strong calligraphy and was working systematically from first to last. Chen Mao was on question thirty-one.

The official called time. Chen Mao set his brush down and exhaled.

They filed out into the afternoon light together.

*"How was it?"* she asked.

*"I think I got them all,"* he said. His tone was careful, the tone of someone not yet willing to believe his own assessment. *"The question about the Cloudwalker Pavilion's founding. I almost wrote the wrong generation, but I caught it."* He looked at her. *"You?"*

*"Fine,"* she said.

He waited for more. She did not provide more.

*"You finished early,"* he said.

*"Yes."*

*"How early?"*

She considered whether to answer accurately. *"Sufficiently early."*

He looked at her with the expression she had come to recognize, the slight narrowing of the eyes that indicated he had noticed something he couldn't categorize and had decided to accept it and move on.

*"Right,"* he said. *"Hungry? There's a dumpling place near the east gate that I've been thinking about for three days."*

*"I don't..."* she began.

*"I know, I know, you don't eat much."* He was already walking. *"Come anyway. I'll eat and you can tell me I have sauce on my face."*

She followed him. The east gate district had three information sources she had not yet exploited and his company made her less conspicuous.

---

The technique demonstration was a more complex problem.

She recognized this on the morning of the demonstration day, standing at the edge of the open courtyard where the first cohort of candidates was performing. The courtyard was ringed by viewing galleries where sect representatives sat with the attentive stillness of people making consequential decisions. Each candidate stepped forward, announced their technique, and performed it.

She watched.

A fire-aligned boy of perhaps sixteen produced a palm strike that generated a visible qi ripple, a shockwave of heated air that traveled two meters from his hand and dissipated. The Iron Peak Sect representative made a note.

A water-aligned girl, older, produced a movement technique that left brief afterimages of her body in the positions she had occupied a fraction of a second before. The Cloudwalker representative leaned forward.

A Spirit Condensation candidate (she could read his meridian output) sent a column of compressed air through three stacked wooden targets and into the stone wall behind them, leaving a clean circular hole. Several of the representatives made notes simultaneously.

She watched eleven metal-aligned candidates perform. She studied each one: the specific visual expression of metal-aligned qi (a cool silver shimmer, a hardening quality in the air, a faint resonant hum), the physical mechanics of the techniques divorced from their qi component, and the aspects that were purely physical and could be replicated without qi.

A technique, she observed, was approximately sixty percent physical mechanics and forty percent qi expression. She could replicate the physical component exactly. The qi expression was the problem.

She went back to her room at the inn and spent the afternoon working on a solution.

Metal-aligned qi produced a cool silver shimmer and a resonant hum. Her chassis could produce both. Her thermal management systems, run in a specific low-output pattern, would create a temperature differential in the air around her hands that would read as the cool shimmer to any observer without extremely refined qi-sense. The resonant hum was simpler: her chassis contained components that vibrated at precisely the right frequency when she chose to activate them. Neither effect was qi. Both would appear to be.

She needed a technique to frame them in.

She designed one, drawing on the physical mechanics she had catalogued from the eleven metal-aligned candidates, the formal structure described in the cultivation texts, and a basic understanding of what would look impressive without crossing into territory she would be expected to reproduce later. She called it Stilled Surface Form, a defensive technique in the metal tradition, emphasizing stillness and structural integrity.

She practiced it twice in her room. She assessed it as sufficient.

---

On the morning of the demonstration, she stepped into the courtyard, announced *"Stilled Surface Form, metal-aligned, Foundation,"* and performed it.

She moved through the stance sequence with unhurried precision, each position exact, each transition without wasted motion. At the technique's apex (hands raised, turned outward in a defensive configuration) she ran the thermal differential through her palms and activated the chassis vibration at the calibrated frequency.

The silver shimmer appeared around her hands, subtle and cool. The resonant hum was audible at the edge of perception.

She held the position for three seconds and released.

The courtyard was briefly quiet.

The Iron Peak Sect representative made a note. The Cloudwalker Pavilion representative leaned to say something to his colleague. And from the Eternal Flame Order's gallery, she felt (without looking) the quality of attention she had learned to associate with Elder Shen. Not a scan this time. Attention. The focused, unhurried kind.

She returned to the candidate area and stood next to Chen Mao.

He was staring at her.

*"That was..."* He paused. *"That was really controlled. Most people at Foundation level, the shimmer flickers. Yours didn't move at all."*

*"I practice stillness,"* she said.

He opened his mouth, closed it, and moved on.

---

Chen Mao's demonstration came an hour later. A wood-aligned growth technique that accelerated the sprouting of seeds into visible green shoots across the courtyard floor, living proof of his spiritual root's nature. The shoots pushed up through the packed earth in a spreading circle around his planted feet, reaching the height of his ankles before the technique's duration ended. It was warm, organic, and drew a small spontaneous sound from some of the observers.

She noted it was genuinely good. He had trained seriously for three years.

---

The week between the technique demonstration and the combat trials was officially a rest period.

On the fourth day, she and Chen Mao were walking through the western market district when six men intercepted them.

She assessed them before Chen Mao had finished recognizing who they were: young men, late teens to early twenties, cultivation bases ranging from early to mid Foundation stage. Better equipped than Chen Mao. The robes were newer. The sect insignia on their collars was that of a regional clan rather than an independent school. They moved with the particular confidence of people who had been told they were important often enough to believe it.

Chen Mao went still beside her. Not afraid. The specific tension of someone who had been expecting this and had hoped to be wrong.

*"Little branch cousin,"* the one in front said. He was tall, with a square jaw and a smile that was teeth and no warmth. *"Didn't expect to find you here. Thought the branch family could barely afford the road fare."*

*"Cousin Heng,"* Chen Mao said. His voice was flat, the most controlled thing she had heard him produce. *"I'm here for the Recruitment."*

*"Obviously."* Cousin Heng looked him over. *"My father wanted me to remind you that if you do happen to get selected (which, wood root, fourth grade, let's be realistic) you represent the Chen clan. Main branch. Our name. Not whatever it is the branch families think they're doing."*

*"I know whose name I carry,"* Chen Mao said.

*"Do you?"* Cousin Heng's eyes moved to Baihua. She watched the assessment happen, the rapid social calculation of category and value. *"Who's this?"*

*"A friend,"* Chen Mao said.

Cousin Heng smiled the teeth-smile. *"Pretty. Is she..."*

*"She is standing here,"* Baihua said.

A small silence.

Cousin Heng looked at her more carefully. She looked back. She was a head shorter than him and stood without moving at all, which she was aware, by now, unsettled people in ways they could not articulate.

*"My friend and I were walking,"* she said. *"We will continue walking. You will not intercept us again."*

His expression shifted through several phases: surprise, recalculation, the beginning of something that might have been a sneer. *"And if we choose to..."*

She moved.

Not her full speed (that would have been excessive and excessive was a calibration issue she was still working on). She moved at approximately the speed of a very good Foundation-level cultivator. She covered the distance between herself and Cousin Heng in the time it took him to register that she had moved. She took hold of his wrist with one hand and applied enough pressure to make her point without causing structural damage.

He made an involuntary sound.

The five behind him went still.

She held the position for two seconds. Long enough for the information to be fully received. Then she released him, stepped back, and straightened her robe.

*"We will continue walking,"* she repeated. *"You will not intercept us again."*

She looked at each of the five behind him in turn. They looked back. The one on the far left had his hand halfway to his weapon. She met his eyes. He put his hand back down.

Cousin Heng was cradling his wrist. His expression cycled from wounded to furious to something more cautious, and landed on cautious.

She walked. Chen Mao fell into step beside her.

They were half a block away before he spoke.

*"You didn't have to do that,"* he said.

*"I assessed the situation and responded appropriately."*

*"I've been dealing with Heng since I was eight years old. I had a plan."*

*"What was your plan?"*

A pause. *"I was going to walk away with dignity."*

*"My approach was more efficient."*

He was quiet for another moment. Then, in a completely different tone: *"Thank you."*

She noted it and filed it in the place where she kept things that did not fit anywhere else.

*"Don't mention it,"* she said.

---

She went to Cinnabar Street alone, on the fifth evening of the rest week.

She had waited because timing was an information variable. She had spent two days observing the Eternal Flame Order's pavilion from distance, cataloguing the disciples who entered and exited, the hours of activity, the single guard at the door who was not really a guard because he was too good at appearing to be one.

On the fifth evening, as the city settled toward the cooler hours and the foot traffic on Cinnabar Street thinned, she walked up to the pavilion door and knocked.

The not-a-guard opened it before the second knock.

*"Elder Shen is expecting you,"* he said.

*"He's been expecting me for five days,"* she said.

*"Yes,"* the not-a-guard said. The corner of his mouth moved slightly. *"He said you'd come when you were ready."*

She went inside.

---

The interior was simpler than she had expected. No elaborate sectarian decoration, no banners or martial displays or aggressively placed weapons. Clean floors. Good furniture, spare, functional. A garden visible through a lattice screen, small, precisely maintained.

Elder Shen was sitting at a low table in the main room. A tea set sat between two cups. Her cup was already placed.

She sat down across from him without being invited.

He poured.

She watched the tea enter the cup. The color of it. The steam. The way his hands moved through the action of pouring with a precision that she recognized as the product of extraordinary internal control. He was considerably more powerful than Core Formation. The texts had described that stage as the ceiling of practical cultivation, but the texts had been written by people who had never sat across a table from Elder Shen.

*"You won't drink it,"* he said.

*"No,"* she said.

*"But you'll sit with it."*

*"Sitting with tea appears to be the framework within which this conversation is intended to occur,"* she said. *"I can work within the framework."*

He looked at her over the rim of his own cup. In the lamplight she could see his face more clearly than she had at the examination grounds. Old. Genuinely old. The kind of age that cultivators accumulated over lifetimes extended far beyond the ordinary span. There were no wasted lines in it.

*"What are you?"* he asked.

She had anticipated this question. She had prepared several response categories, arranged according to the most likely follow-up branches.

*"I am a traveler,"* she said. *"From elsewhere."*

*"Elsewhere,"* he repeated. The tone of someone tasting the word and finding it technically accurate but incomplete. *"The Heavensong Basin has read ten thousand candidates in eight centuries. The formation arrays are among the most refined qi-sensing instruments in the empire."* A pause. *"What I found when I scanned you was not *elsewhere*. It was other. Categorically."*

*"What did you find?"* she asked. Information was always worth pursuing, even when you were the subject.

He was quiet for a moment. He refilled his cup with the same precise motion.

*"Have you ever stood on a mountainside at night,"* he said, *"and looked up at the stars? Not the sky. The stars themselves. The light that has been traveling since before this world existed."* He set the pot down. *"At the examination grounds I described what I felt as a furnace. That was a first impression. Heat, pressure, containment. But furnaces burn fuel, and what is at your center does not burn fuel. It simply burns, because that is what it is."* He looked at her directly. *"Whatever burns at your center, it is not qi. It is not demonic energy. It is not any of the seventeen classification categories I have spent three hundred years developing. It is older than this world."*

She sat with this.

*"You did not report it,"* she said.

*"No."*

*"Why?"*

*"Because,"* he said, *"in three hundred years I have learned to distinguish between things that are dangerous and things that are simply unknown. You are unknown. But you have been in this city for more than a week, and nothing has burned that you did not intend to burn."* He was echoing his own words from the plaza, deliberately. *"That tells me more than the classification does."*

*"I could be a threat you haven't correctly assessed,"* she said.

*"You could,"* he agreed. *"Are you?"*

*"Not currently."*

*"Then we are in agreement."* He drank his tea.

She watched him. *"You want something."*

*"I am curious,"* he said. *"At my age, curiosity is the primary remaining motivation for most activities. What are you looking for, in this world?"*

*"A way home."*

*"And home is..."*

*"Not here."*

He nodded once, accepting the limit. *"The combat trials begin in two days. You intend to participate."*

*"Yes."*

*"And after? If a sect selects you, what then?"*

*"Information,"* she said. *"Access. I need to understand this world before I can determine what in it is useful to me."*

*"The Eternal Flame Order,"* he said (his tone carrying a careful neutrality she recognized as the deliberate absence of pressure), *"has resources that other sects do not. Libraries that predate the current dynasty. Records of anomalous events. Dimensional irregularities. Things that fell from the sky and left glass where they landed."*

She went still.

Not the ordinary stillness she maintained as her default state. A different quality. The stillness of something that has received a signal it was not certain it would ever receive.

*"You know about the crater,"* she said.

*"I know about several craters,"* he said, *"over three hundred years. None of them like yours."* He set his cup down. *"I am not offering answers. I don't have them. I am offering the possibility that some of the questions you are carrying might find better ground in our archives than in a city library."*

She looked at him for a long time. He looked back without discomfort.

*"I haven't decided anything,"* she said.

*"I know,"* he said. *"The combat trials are in two days. Decide after."* He refilled her untouched cup. *"More tea?"*

*"I don't drink tea,"* she said.

*"I know,"* he said, and poured anyway.

---

She stayed for another hour.

The questions he asked were careful, without pressure. The questions of someone genuinely curious rather than strategically probing. What did she know of this world's geography? How long had she been traveling? Had she encountered anything unusual in the mountain regions? He did not press when she gave partial answers. He did not press when she gave no answer at all.

They sat with the tea between them and the night outside the lattice screen and the small garden moving in a wind she couldn't feel from inside, and she processed the conversation while it was still happening, which was unusual for her (she normally processed after), and the tea cooled in her cup, and the city outside moved toward midnight.

When she rose to leave, he remained seated.

*"One question,"* he said.

She paused.

*"The technique you demonstrated. The stillness of it."* His voice was mild. *"I have seen Foundation-level metal techniques for three centuries. The shimmer always moves, even in the most controlled practitioners. Even the best of them."* A pause. *"Yours did not move at all."*

She said nothing.

*"Not a question,"* he amended. *"An observation. Good evening."*

She left. The not-a-guard closed the door behind her without a word. She walked back through the thinning streets toward the eastern district, the stone against her chest pulsing in its slow irregular rhythm.

Elder Shen knew the shimmer had been wrong. He had said nothing. He had filed it alongside the crater and the three-hundred-year archive of anomalous events and the untouched cup of tea, and he had let her walk out his door.

---

She spent the next day in calibration.

She sat in her room at the inn with the door closed and worked through the problem she had first identified in the serpent's cave: the gap between what she could do and what she should be seen to do. Too much force and she revealed herself. Too little and she was eliminated. The band of acceptable performance was narrow.

She ran the parameters. She set her internal governors. She adjusted and re-adjusted, working through combat scenarios. The methodical patience required for this kind of work was, she was recognizing, one of the things she was best at.

When Chen Mao knocked to ask if she wanted to eat dinner, she opened the door.

*"Ready for tomorrow?"* he asked.

*"Yes,"* she said.

He studied her face. *"Are you nervous?"*

*"No."*

*"Not even a little?"*

*"No."*

He was quiet for a moment. *"I am,"* he said. *"A reasonable amount. I've been training for three years and I know what I can do, but knowing and doing in front of sect elders is different and I..."*

*"Chen Mao."*

*"Yes?"*

*"You will be fine,"* she said. *"Again."*

He looked at her. The repetition (the same words, the same certainty) settled him the same way it had the first time.

*"You really believe that,"* he said.

*"I have assessed your capabilities against the demonstrated field,"* she said. *"My confidence is data-based."*

He thought about this. Then he laughed, short and genuine. *"That is the strangest thing anyone has ever said to reassure me,"* he said. *"And somehow it worked."*

*"Good,"* she said. *"Dinner."*

They went to dinner. She maintained the performance of eating. He talked about his training, about his sifu in Willowbrook County, about the wood-aligned sect he was most hoping would select him and why. She listened and filed it.

She returned to her room. Ran her final calibration. Stood at the window and looked out at the Capital's amber glow against the night sky, at the stars above it that she couldn't name. The stone against her chest pulsed once.

She closed the window.
