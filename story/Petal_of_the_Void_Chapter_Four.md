# PETAL OF THE VOID

## Chapter Four: The City That Touches Heaven

---

She was three li out when she saw the light above the walls.

Not the walls themselves, not yet. The light. A warm ruddy glow that turned the clouds overhead to copper even in the middle of the afternoon. The city was burning with its own illumination, thousands of lanterns and hearthfires and the qi-saturated architecture of a place that had been accumulating spiritual energy for centuries. All of it leaked upward into the sky, visible from distance.

The road had grown steadily more crowded over the last two days of travel across the Thousand Li Plains. Merchants with loaded carts. Pilgrims in worn robes. Families with children in tow, the children restless, the parents tired. And increasing numbers of young people traveling alone or in small groups, their pace faster than the merchants', their packs lighter, their postures carrying a specific tension she had catalogued over the past day. Calluses on their hands from training. The occasional flicker of qi she could detect with her sensors as a faint thermal variation around a person's meridian lines.

She walked among them. The crowd thickened as the road approached the city, and by the final li the road was full, bodies packed close, everyone moving at the speed of the slowest cart.

The city gates were enormous. Forty meters high. Red lacquered timber and black iron, the surface of each gate panel carved with characters she could read (the formal declaration of imperial authority over the Vermillion Capital). Flanking towers flew banners she identified against her library readings: the imperial gold-and-crimson in the center, and on either side the sect standards, each one different, twelve of them in total.

*Seven major sects represented. Three she had no prior data on.*

She stood in the queue of arrivals, catalogued the banners, and waited her turn. The gate guards here were more numerous and more attentive than Silverwind's, six of them visible at the gate itself and another four in the towers above, but the volume of arrivals made individual scrutiny impractical. They waved people through in groups of five and six.

She walked through the gate and into the Vermillion Capital.

---

The city was larger than Silverwind by an order of magnitude.

Districts folded into districts, each with its own character. The imperial district rose in tiered terraces toward the north, the architecture growing more ornate with altitude, rooflines curling upward at their corners. The market districts spread outward from the central avenues in a dense grid that her mapping systems processed as she walked, building the picture street by street. Temples appeared every few blocks, their incense smoke rising to join the general haze above the rooftops.

The cultivators were everywhere.

She identified them by the thermal signature of their meridian activity, by the way ordinary citizens gave them slightly more space without appearing to do so consciously, by the occasional shimmer of released qi that attended certain gestures. Foundation-stage, most of them. A handful that read as Spirit Condensation. And twice, in the first two hours of walking, figures in sect robes whose presence spiked her threat sensors and made her slow her pace to observe from distance. Their meridian readings exceeded the parameters she had been calibrating against. Core Formation, at minimum.

She did not approach them. She watched, catalogued, and continued.

---

She found lodging in the eastern district, a clean inn with a private room on the second floor. The proprietor was a middle-aged man who looked at her coins, counted them, and handed her a key without comment.

She spent the first two days mapping the city the same way she had mapped Silverwind, in expanding spirals from her lodging, each circuit taking in more territory. The Capital's information density was substantially higher. The libraries were larger (she found three in the first day and read them all). The teahouses were full of cultivators in transit whose conversations contained technical detail she could now follow with increasing precision.

On the second day she located the sect pavilions that had been erected near the imperial examination grounds. Seven pavilions, each displaying their sect's colors and insignia. She catalogued each one, noting the guard patterns, the foot traffic, the quality of the cultivators who entered and exited.

On the third day, she was sitting at a noodle stall in the eastern market district, maintaining the social performance of eating a bowl of noodles while her auditory processing swept the surrounding conversations, when someone sat down on the bench beside her.

---

*"You're here for the Recruitment, right?"*

She turned and looked at him.

He was young, perhaps eighteen, a year or two younger than she presented. Average height, broad-shouldered, with an open face and hair that had been carefully arranged at some point earlier in the day and had since abandoned all discipline. His robe was clean but worn at the cuffs, the kind of wear that came from long travel and frequent use. He was holding a bowl of noodles in both hands and looking at her with the uncomplicated friendliness of a person who sat down next to strangers and started talking as a matter of course.

She ran her standard assessment. No threat indicators. Elevated heart rate (excitement, not aggression). Calluses consistent with several years of basic martial training. Faint meridian activity suggesting a modest but genuine qi root.

*"Yes,"* she said, and returned to her noodles.

*"Me too."* He said this with genuine enthusiasm, as though the fact of shared purpose were itself noteworthy. *"I'm Chen Mao. From Willowbrook County, it's about eight hundred li southwest of here, you probably haven't heard of it. I've been training for three years. My sifu says I have a wood-aligned root, which isn't the most impressive but it's solid, he says it's solid."* A pause while he ate noodles. *"You?"*

*"Baihua. From the west."*

*"Just... the west?"*

*"Yes."*

He accepted this without pressing. *"Are you nervous? I'm nervous. I mean, I'm not nervous, I've trained for this, but I'm..."* He reconsidered. *"I'm a little nervous."*

*"I am not nervous,"* she said.

*"How are you not nervous? There are going to be sect elders there. Actual sect elders. From the Iron Peak Sect and the Cloudwalker Pavilion and the..."* He lowered his voice. *"I heard the Eternal Flame Order is sending a Grand Elder this cycle. That almost never happens."*

*"Tell me about the Recruitment structure,"* she said.

He blinked at the redirect. Then he shrugged and told her.

---

The Recruitment process had four stages. Chen Mao described each one with the fluency of someone who had been thinking about this event for three years and had memorized every available detail.

*"First is the Spiritual Root Assessment,"* he said, counting on his fingers. *"Everyone goes before the Heavensong Basin. It's this jade basin the imperial family has had for eight hundred years. It reads your qi root and displays it. Root quality, elemental alignment, grade. If you don't have a root, or if your root is below fourth grade, you're out. That's where most people get eliminated. Eighty percent, roughly."*

*"And if you pass the Assessment?"*

*"Three tests. First is a written examination, cultivation theory, history of the major sects, basic formations knowledge. Second is a demonstration, you perform whatever technique you've mastered and the sect representatives grade it. And the third..."* His expression shifted, excitement and apprehension in equal measure. *"Combat. Single elimination. You fight until you lose or until a sect picks you."*

*"Sects choose disciples based on combat performance?"*

*"Combat and the other tests together. But combat is the one that gets attention."* He ate more noodles. *"Different sects weight things differently. Cloudwalker Pavilion cares about the theory exam. Iron Peak Sect basically only cares about combat. The Eternal Flame Order..."* He lowered his voice again. *"They're the ones nobody understands. They've taken disciples with mediocre roots before, if something about the person interested them."*

She noted the Eternal Flame Order with additional attention.

*"What happens to disciples after they're selected?"*

*"They go back to the sect. Train for years. Some of them come back as..."* He gestured at the sect-robed figures moving through the market crowd. *"That."*

She nodded once and returned to the performance of eating.

Chen Mao stayed at the stall for another hour. He talked about his training, his sifu, his journey from Willowbrook County, the three inns he had stayed at along the way and the quality of the food at each one. She listened with half her processing attention and used the rest to continue building her tactical picture.

When she stood to leave, he stood too.

*"Which way are you going?"* he asked.

*"East,"* she said.

*"Me too,"* he said, and fell into step beside her.

She looked at him. He looked back. No guile. No agenda.

*"You don't know where I'm going,"* she said.

*"East, you said."*

She ran the calculation. He was a local information source with three years of Recruitment-specific knowledge. His social ease made people talk to him. He had no apparent agenda beyond being friendly.

*"Don't get in the way,"* she said, and walked east.

He grinned and followed.

---

He did not get in the way.

Over the next four days they moved through the city together. He talked. She listened. His presence at her side produced a social effect she had not anticipated: people were more forthcoming with two travelers than with one, particularly when one of them smiled easily and asked questions with genuine interest. She began using him as a social interface. He appeared content with this arrangement.

On the second day of their partnership, they were sitting in a teahouse near the Iron Peak Sect's pavilion when Chen Mao struck up a conversation with a city guard's son at the next table. In twenty minutes of casual exchange, she learned: the Iron Peak Sect and the Cloudwalker Pavilion had a grudge running back forty years over a territorial boundary in the northern mountains that both parties maintained was resolved and neither party had resolved. The Eternal Flame Order's Grand Elder (referred to in hushed tones as *Elder Shen*) had arrived three days ago and had not been seen since, which was considered notable.

On the fifth day, Chen Mao came back from a wine shop with specific intelligence. The monster activity she had heard about in Silverwind City had worsened. Three more caravans lost on the western route. Something had been sighted near the third mountain pass. Sect disciples had been dispatched, returned, and filed a report that had been classified above standard access.

She filed this. The pattern was consistent. The escalation was significant. She set it aside for later and returned her attention to the immediate problem.

---

The Heavensong Basin.

She had found a description of it in the Capital's central archive: a jade basin approximately one meter across, filled with water drawn from a spiritual spring and treated with eight centuries of accumulated formation work. A cultivator placed their hands on the rim. The basin read their qi root and displayed the result as a column of colored light above the water's surface, height indicating grade, color indicating elemental alignment.

The problem was clear. She had no qi root. She had a fusion core. If the basin read what was actually inside her, the result would either register as nothing (disqualifying) or as something that would draw exactly the kind of attention she was trying to avoid.

She spent an afternoon in her room at the inn, working through the problem.

She ran through her available systems. Her undamaged capabilities. The tools she had that this world would have no framework for.

The answer surfaced from a sub-system she had not yet had reason to access. A peripheral utility, small and specialized: nanoscale maintenance units. A colony of them, housed in a reservoir behind her left clavicle, designed for internal repair work on components too small or delicate for mechanical intervention. They had been working passively since the crash, slowly repairing memory fragmentation, damaged sensor arrays, microfractures in her chassis plating.

She could also direct them externally.

The Heavensong Basin was, at its fundamental level, a sensor. It read qi signatures. Sensors could be given false readings, if you understood what signal they were looking for and could generate a convincing version of it.

She queried her baseline qi-field readings. She had been cataloguing the ambient qi of this world for two weeks. She understood the signature of a wood-aligned qi root well enough, having spent four days walking next to one. Chen Mao's meridian activity had been a thorough (if inadvertent) reference sample.

She could synthesize a different signature. Metal-aligned, fifth grade. Uncommon enough to be interesting, not so uncommon as to be suspicious. Her nanobots could carry the synthesized signature to the basin's rim and hold it there for the few seconds needed.

She spent the evening refining the synthesis parameters, running calibration tests against her own sensor array. When Chen Mao knocked on her door to ask if she wanted to come look at the lantern market, she went. The lantern market had no operational value, but it was a reasonable use of the remaining evening, and she had been standing in her room working on nanobot frequency calibration for four hours.

---

The morning of the Spiritual Root Assessment.

The city was louder than usual. She could hear it from her room at the inn before she opened the door: elevated ambient noise, the sound of thousands of people converging on the examination grounds. She dressed, checked her nanobot deployment parameters one final time, and went to meet Chen Mao in the inn's main hall.

He was already there, sitting at a table with a bowl of congee he was not eating. His robe collar was disarranged. He was tapping one foot on the floor in a rapid rhythm he did not appear to be aware of.

*"What element do you think yours will be?"* he asked.

*"Unknown,"* she said.

*"I hope you get something good. Metal is impressive. Fire is impressive. Wood is underrated, I think, people underestimate..."*

*"Chen Mao."*

*"Yes?"*

*"You will be fine."*

He looked at her. Something in her tone (not warm, but certain, the certainty of a factual statement) settled him. He nodded. He rearranged his collar. He stood up.

They joined the queue.

---

The examination grounds were a broad stone plaza in the shadow of the imperial district. Tiered viewing galleries surrounded the plaza on three sides, already filling with observers. At the center, on a raised dais, the Heavensong Basin sat in its cradle. Jade-green, ancient, its rim worn smooth.

Around the dais stood the sect representatives, men and women in various colors of formal robes, their expressions evaluative. She catalogued them as she approached, cross-referencing against her data.

Among them, standing slightly apart, hands folded in his sleeves, in robes the deep orange-red of banked coals: an old man.

Her sensors spiked.

*Thermal reading: anomalous. Meridian activity: beyond standard sensor calibration range. Classification: Core Formation minimum. Possibly higher.*

Elder Shen.

He was not watching anyone in particular. He was watching everyone, his eyes moving across the queue of waiting candidates with an expression of calm attention. She noted his position relative to the Basin (twelve meters, line of sight unobstructed) and his distance from her current position in the queue (approximately forty meters) and continued forward.

---

The queue moved in steady increments. She observed the assessment process for forty minutes before her turn arrived.

Each candidate approached the Basin, placed their hands on the rim, and waited. The column of light rose from the water's surface. An official with a recording brush called out the result: element, grade. The candidate either stepped to the passing side of the plaza (relief visible in their posture, their breathing, their suddenly loosened shoulders) or was directed to the elimination queue (devastation, some of them, tears, one young woman who stood motionless for several seconds before the official gently touched her arm and pointed her toward the exit).

Chen Mao was three places ahead of her. He stepped up to the Basin. He placed his hands on the rim. The column rose: green-gold, a solid meter and a half.

*"Wood-aligned, fourth grade,"* the official called.

Chen Mao pumped his fist once, small, controlled. He stepped to the passing side and stood there with his eyes bright, his breathing elevated, his whole body carrying the specific tension of someone who had just achieved something they had been working toward for three years.

She stepped up to the Basin.

The jade was cool under her fingertips. She felt the formation arrays activate, a faint resonance moving through the rim, the Basin reaching for her qi signature.

She gave it what it was looking for.

The nanobots moved through her fingertips in a stream invisible to any eye in this world, dispersing in a thin film across the rim's inner surface, generating the synthesized metal-aligned signature at the precise parameters she had calibrated the night before. The Basin read what they told it to read.

The column of light rose. Pale silver-white. A meter and three quarters. Metal-aligned. Fifth grade.

A murmur moved through the nearby observers. The recording official's brush paused for a fraction of a second before marking it down. She withdrew her hands, recalled the nanobots in a single quiet pulse, and stepped to the passing side.

Chen Mao was at her elbow immediately.

*"Fifth grade metal. That's... Baihua, that's really good, the Iron Peak Sect is going to..."*

*"Later,"* she said.

She had felt something. At the moment the column of light rose, in the space between one heartbeat and the next, she had detected a secondary scan. Faint. Refined. Moving through the air with a subtlety that her sensors had almost missed.

It had come from the direction of the old man in orange-red robes.

She did not look at him. She stood beside Chen Mao and watched the next candidate approach the Basin and kept her attention on the periphery of her sensor range, where Elder Shen stood with his hands in his sleeves and his expression unchanged.

For thirty seconds, nothing happened.

Then he moved.

---

He crossed the plaza without apparent haste. His path was a gentle arc that could have been aimed at any of a dozen destinations. He came to rest approximately two meters to her left, facing the Basin, apparently watching the ongoing assessments with the same general attention he had been applying to everything.

She did not look at him. He did not look at her.

*"Interesting weather lately,"* he said, in a voice pitched for two meters, conversational, almost bored. *"I find the air near the mountains has a particular quality this time of year."*

The words were not directed at her. They were directed at the general air in front of him.

She said nothing.

*"Like standing near a furnace,"* he continued, in the same tone, *"and yet somehow the furnace has never once burned anything it did not intend to burn."* A pause. *"Remarkable restraint, for a furnace."*

Chen Mao, at her right elbow, was watching the next candidate's assessment and not listening. She was certain of this.

She kept her gaze on the Basin.

After a moment, Elder Shen produced a small folded paper from his sleeve, studied it as though it contained something important, and spoke one final time, barely above a murmur.

*"The Eternal Flame Order's pavilion is on Cinnabar Street. The door is open to those who find their way there."*

He refolded the paper. He walked away. He did not look back.

She tracked him with her sensors until he reached the edge of the plaza and disappeared into the crowd. Then she stood with Chen Mao's voice running beside her (something about the Iron Peak Sect's famous intake selection process) and processed what had occurred.

He had scanned her. She had felt it. He had found something that bore no resemblance to a fifth-grade metal root, or to any human cultivator she suspected he had encountered in three hundred years of encountering them. And he had said nothing to the officials. He had not raised an alarm.

He had told her where to find him.

She would go to Cinnabar Street. On her own terms. With full situational awareness. She would decide what to reveal and what to withhold when she got there.

*"...and the Iron Peak inner disciples apparently do three hundred forms every morning before breakfast,"* Chen Mao was saying, *"which sounds excessive to me but I suppose that's why they're inner disciples..."*

*"Chen Mao,"* she said.

*"Yes?"*

*"You passed."*

He stopped talking. He looked at his hands. He looked at the passing side of the plaza where they were standing. He looked at her.

*"I passed,"* he said.

*"Yes."*

*"I actually..."* He needed a moment. *"I've been training for three years and I just..."*

*"Yes."*

He grinned. Wide, unguarded, without performance. She noted it and filed it in the place where she kept things that did not fit anywhere else.

Around them the queue moved on. The Basin read each new candidate in turn. Across the plaza, Elder Shen walked toward Cinnabar Street with his hands in his sleeves.
