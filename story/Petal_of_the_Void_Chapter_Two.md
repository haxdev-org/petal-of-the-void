# PETAL OF THE VOID

## Chapter Two: A City of Paper and Steel

---

Silverwind City announced itself before it came into view.

She smelled it first: woodsmoke, roasting meat, the fermented sharpness of grain wine, and underneath all of that something earthier, denser, the accumulated output of ten thousand people living in proximity behind walls. The smell reached her when she was still in the tree cover on the last ridge, and she stopped walking for two seconds to isolate and categorize each component before continuing.

Then the sound. A low continuous murmur, audible from half a li out, the kind of sound a settlement made when it never fully stopped being active. She could pick out individual threads within it: a cart wheel on stone, a voice raised in argument, the rhythmic clang of a hammer on metal.

Then, cresting the ridge as the sun broke above the eastern peaks, she saw the city itself.

Grey stone walls, old, well-maintained, rising approximately eight meters at the gatehouse and tapering to six along the curtain walls between towers. Inside those walls, rooftops cascaded downhill in overlapping tiers of dark clay tile, interrupted at intervals by the upswept eaves of larger buildings. She identified a temple by its incense-smoke, a market hall by its noise signature, and a tall narrow structure near the center that was probably administrative. Banners hung from the gatehouse, red and gold, snapping in the morning wind. A queue of merchants with carts and mules stretched back from the gate along the road, moving slowly, stopping, moving again.

She studied it for four seconds, catalogued what she could, and walked down toward the queue.

---

The queue moved in short increments. She stood behind a grain merchant whose cart was loaded with sacks of millet. He did not look at her. The merchant ahead of him, a woman with two mules carrying bolts of undyed cloth, glanced back once, assessed the silk robe, and turned forward again.

When she reached the gate, two guards in leather armor looked at her. One of them, the shorter one, let his eyes move from her face to the robe to her hands (no calluses, no visible weapons) and back. He waved her through without a word.

She had expected questions, a lone woman arriving at dawn with no cart and no companions, but the robe had done something she had not explicitly planned for. The guards had seen the silk and drawn a conclusion. She walked through the gate and into the city.

*Clothing as social signal. High utility. Maintain.*

---

The streets inside were narrow. The buildings were timber-framed, their upper floors extending out over the street, each one reaching further than the one below it until the sky above was reduced to a bright strip between eaves. She walked slowly, mapping as she went.

Shopfronts opened directly onto the street, their proprietors setting out wares for the day. A cloth merchant was arranging bolts of fabric on a wooden rack, greens and blues and one deep red that caught the morning light. Across from him, an herbalist was hanging bundles of dried plants from hooks above her door, each bundle tied with colored thread. She identified twelve of the plant species from their chemical signatures and found matches for nine of them in the natural philosophy texts she had not yet read.

Further along the street: a cage-maker, with cages of various sizes containing birds she had no reference for (one of them, pale blue with a crest of gold, was singing in a pitch her auditory array registered as pleasantly structured). A baker, pulling rounds of flatbread from a clay oven and stacking them on a board set across two barrels. Children, three of them, running between the legs of a man carrying a basket of oranges, their voices high and fast, their language the same one she had learned from the bandits but spoken at a pace that tested her tonal resolution.

She continued walking.

An old man sat outside a teahouse near the second intersection, his eyes closed, a cup of something steaming held in both hands. A woman swept the steps of a house with a broom made of bundled grass. Two men were arguing about the price of lamp oil at a stall where the oil was displayed in glass bottles of varying clarity.

She noted all of it, filed all of it, and kept moving.

Her priority queue was clear. She needed three things: information about this world's geography, political structure, and threat landscape. A method of acquiring local currency or resources (she had the bandits' coins, but did not know how far they would go). And a better understanding of the phenomenon she had observed the night before, the serpentine shape in the clouds above the forest, and the references in the bandit's stolen documents to something called *cultivated power*.

The documents had been fragmentary, a merchant's letter referencing a *sect escort* as though martial protection from supernatural threats were an ordinary shipping expense. She had flagged it. She flagged everything she didn't understand, and that file was growing.

She stopped at the next intersection and sampled the air. Paper. Old paper, the specific chemical signature of cellulose in slow decomposition, strong enough to indicate a significant concentration of stored documents nearby. She followed it east for two blocks, turned left, and found the source.

---

The sign above the door read, in the formal script she was still calibrating to, *Hall of Preserved Words*. She translated it, considered the translation, and decided it meant the same thing as *library*.

She went inside.

The interior was a single large room, the ceiling a good four meters up, with shelves running from floor to ceiling on three walls. Ladders on rolling tracks provided access to the upper shelves, the tracks mounted on iron rails, the whole system clearly the work of someone who had found the engineering problem more interesting than the solution strictly required. Scrolls in lacquered tubes occupied the upper shelves. Bound volumes with wooden covers filled the middle sections. Loose papers pressed between boards and weighted with stones sat on the lower shelves near the reading tables.

A small old woman sat behind a desk near the entrance. She had the specific quality of someone who had been sitting in that position for a very long time and intended to continue. She looked up when the door opened and her expression did not change.

*"Scholar rate or browsing rate?"* she said.

She set three copper coins on the desk. She had studied the currency from the bandits' packs during the walk, comparing denominations, weights, and the stamped faces on each type. Three copper appeared to be the level for minor transactions.

*"Browsing,"* she said. *"I am newly arrived in the city. I am unfamiliar with the regional holdings."*

The old woman looked at the coins. She looked at the person who had placed them. She picked up the coins and put them in a wooden box behind the desk.

*"Geography, history, and civic records, left side,"* she said, pointing. *"Natural philosophy and medicine, back wall. The Dao texts and cultivation theory are in the restricted section."* She paused. *"That costs considerably more."*

*"Cultivation theory?"*

*"Considerably more,"* the woman repeated, and returned to her ledger.

She went to the left wall first.

---

She took the first scroll from the geography section, a map of the surrounding prefecture, and unrolled it on the nearest reading table. Her optical array photographed the entire surface in 0.4 seconds, processed the terrain notation, and filed the result. She rolled it up, replaced it, and took the next one.

The second scroll was a regional geography covering the mountain systems to the west and north. She held it for six seconds.

The third was a trade route map with annotations in three different hands, the oldest of which she dated from the ink composition to approximately two hundred years before the present period. Eight seconds.

She moved through the geography section shelf by shelf. Each scroll or volume she picked up, opened, photographed page by page, processed, and replaced in its original position. The physical motions (take, open, hold, close, replace) took longer than the reading. Her optical array ingested each page in less than a second. The cross-referencing and filing ran in parallel while her hands reached for the next volume.

In fourteen minutes she had read the entire geography section.

The picture that assembled itself was of a world that called itself, in its own texts, *the Ten Thousand Li Under Heaven* (not a formal name, more a statement of the obvious). It was large. The political structure was fragmented: a nominal imperial authority centered in a city called the Vermillion Capital (a thousand li northeast), surrounded by semi-autonomous territories, noble houses, and entities the texts referred to as *the Sects*. The Sects appeared to operate outside imperial jurisdiction. The civic history texts she found in the next section phrased this diplomatically. The diplomatic phrasing drew her attention. Something was being carefully not said, and the shape of what was not being said was the shape of organizations more powerful than any government force.

She moved to the history section and spent twenty-two minutes there. The dynastic records were detailed but formulaic, each dynasty's rise and fall described in the same structural pattern. She noted the pattern and extracted the data. The Sects appeared in the margins of every dynastic period, always present, rarely explained, treated as permanent features of the landscape rather than historical actors.

She moved to the back wall.

Natural philosophy. She read the cosmological texts, the medical texts, and a compendium of what the author called *the eight principles of qi interaction*. The cosmology was elaborate and internally consistent but not empirically grounded. She noted it as a working model and moved on.

*Qi* appeared in every text. A fundamental energy, present in all living things and in the environment itself, capable of being cultivated, refined, and used by those with sufficient talent and training. She cross-referenced this against the document fragments from the bandit's packs. She cross-referenced it against the faint residual readings her sensors had been unable to categorize since her arrival, a low-frequency field effect present in the air, in the soil, in everything around her. She cross-referenced it against the moment, the night before, when the serpentine shape had moved through the clouds and her sensors had registered a brief spike in the same unclassifiable field.

She ran the cross-reference three times.

The field effect was real. It was everywhere. The cultivation texts described what people did with it.

She stood up from the reading table, walked to the old woman's desk, and set a silver coin on it.

The old woman looked at the coin. Looked at her. Picked it up.

*"One hour,"* she said.

---

The restricted section was a corner of the room closed off by a folding screen. Behind the screen: two shelves of volumes, organized by what she determined (from labels in a formal hand) to be school and theoretical framework. She sat on the floor because there was no chair and read them all.

She started with a text titled *On the Foundation of the Martial Body*, written in a clear formal hand. The author described Foundation-stage cultivation: the process of drawing qi from the environment into the body, strengthening it beyond ordinary human limits. Faster, stronger, more resilient. She compared the described capabilities against her own performance in the clearing the night before. Foundation-stage cultivators were within the parameters she had already demonstrated.

The next text, *Principles of Spirit Condensation*, described the stage above Foundation. A cultivator who had opened their meridians could channel qi outward. Project force without physical contact. Generate heat or cold. Perceive things beyond ordinary sensory range. The text described adepts who could shatter boulders without touching them, perceive heartbeats at a hundred meters, move faster than a loosed arrow.

She set the text down and picked up the next one.

*On the Formation of the Core.* At this stage, a cultivator compressed their refined qi into a permanent structure inside their body, an inner core that functioned as both reservoir and engine. The text's language shifted here. The author was no longer describing things with clinical detachment. The descriptions of Core Formation cultivators carried a specific weight. They could fly. They could level buildings. They could affect things at a cellular level. The author appeared to have personally witnessed at least some of this and had been marked by the experience.

Beyond Core Formation: stages the texts named but described in language that grew increasingly imprecise. *Nascent Soul. Spirit Severing. Dao Ascension.* The later texts used poetry where the earlier ones used observation. She read this shift as an indicator that the authors had been describing things beyond their own direct experience.

She set down the last text and sat with the information.

Her fusion core output was nominal. She had more than enough raw power for anything she had encountered so far. But raw power and correctly applied power against an opponent who could perceive her at a hundred meters and move faster than an arrow were different problems. She was capable enough to recognize the difference.

She needed more information. She needed to understand these entities at close range, their weaknesses, their organizational structures, their behavior toward a lone traveler who demonstrated capabilities they would not be able to categorize.

She needed someone who would talk.

She replaced the cultivation texts in their positions on the shelves, walked to the old woman's desk, and said *"Thank you."*

The old woman did not look up.

She went to find a tavern.

---

The Silver Wind Pavilion was on the main commercial street in the market district. She identified it as the largest drinking establishment by walking the length of the street and comparing the noise levels coming from each building. The Pavilion was the loudest by a measurable margin.

Inside: a large room, low ceiling, wooden tables, a haze of cooking smoke and lamp oil. It was mid-afternoon and the room was already more than half full. Merchants sat in groups of three and four, arguing over documents spread between cups and plates. Off-duty city guards occupied a long bench near the left wall, their weapons leaning against the wall behind them. In the far corner, a storyteller was performing something elaborate with his hands, his voice rising and falling in dramatic cadences, a small crowd of eight or nine people leaning forward on their stools.

She found a seat at a small table near the back wall. A server, a girl of perhaps fifteen with her hair pulled back in a practical knot, came to the table.

*"What can I get you?"*

*"Tea,"* she said.

The server left. She would not drink the tea. She ordered it because sitting at a table without ordering was conspicuous, and conspicuous was the opposite of her current objective.

She opened her auditory processing to full passive sweep and let it run.

The room was dense with conversation. She processed it in parallel streams, each table's discussion running as a separate channel.

Table nearest the door, two merchants: *"...three caravans in six weeks, I'm telling you. The guild has stopped running the western route entirely..."*

She flagged this. Monster activity. Consistent with what the bandit's documents had referenced.

Table by the left wall, city guards: *"...patrol came back short two men. Zhang won't talk about it. Won't say what they saw. He's drinking his way through his wages and sleeping in the guard barracks because he won't go home to his wife in the dark..."*

She flagged this.

Table near the storyteller, a group of four, younger, animated: *"...Grand Disciple Recruitment, next month. My brother's cousin's friend trained at the Iron Peak auxiliary school and he says this year the Iron Peak Sect is sending three inner disciples to observe..."*

*"Every five years,"* a second voice at the same table. *"My whole life I've been hearing about the Recruitment and it's finally here..."*

She flagged this with particular attention. A major gathering. All the Sects in one place. A concentration of information.

Center table, a merchant in an expensive robe, speaking to anyone who would listen: *"My son has been training since he was six. Six years old, every morning at dawn. If he doesn't pass the sect tests this cycle, I don't know what..."* He trailed off and took a drink from his cup. The man across from him nodded with the patient expression of someone who had heard this before.

Table near the back, two older men, voices low: *"...Emperor's health. Not good. The third prince is making arrangements..."*

*"Arrangements?"*

*"Arrangements. The kind that involve armies."*

She flagged the political intelligence and kept processing.

Twenty minutes. She had a working picture of local concerns. Monster activity in the northern passes (confirmed from multiple sources). The Grand Disciple Recruitment at the Vermillion Capital (confirmed, next month, every five years, all major Sects participating). The Emperor's failing health and succession disputes (useful context, filed for later analysis).

She was processing the fourth concurrent conversation stream when someone sat down across from her.

---

She registered the movement, the displacement of the chair across from her, the placement of a cup on the table, and redirected her attention.

He was young, perhaps twenty. His robe was pale gold silk with embroidery at the collar, the kind of expense that was designed to be noticed. His hair was pinned with a jade ornament. His face had the particular quality of a face that had been told it was handsome many times and had incorporated this assessment as a foundational fact. He set his wine cup down on her table with the precise casualness of a gesture he had performed before and expected a specific response to.

*"A beauty sitting alone,"* he said, with a smile that used his mouth but not the rest of his face, *"is an invitation the heavens themselves could not ignore."*

She looked at him. She ran a rapid assessment: no weapon visible, no calluses on the hands consistent with martial training, elevated heart rate (excitement or nerves), pupils dilated. Non-threatening. Potentially useful as an information source if the interaction could be directed.

*"Sit down,"* she said, *"if you have useful information."*

He blinked. The smile adjusted, a small recalibration, the expression of someone encountering a response that didn't fit his expected set but wasn't entirely outside his recovery range.

*"A direct woman. How refreshing."* He settled into the seat across from her, gestured toward the server for more wine. *"I am Wei Longfeng, third son of Magistrate Wei of Silverwind Prefecture. You are...?"*

*"Passing through,"* she said.

*"A traveler."* He leaned forward. *"You know, this city can be difficult to navigate alone. Particularly for a woman of obvious refinement. I happen to know it well. Every tea house, every garden, every scenic overlook. I would be honored to serve as a guide."*

*"I have already located the library, the market, and this establishment without assistance,"* she said. *"My navigation is adequate."*

The smile held. He was persistent. She noted this as a data point.

*"A learned woman as well. Beauty and scholarship."* He reached across the table and set his hand over hers.

She looked at his hand on hers. She looked at him.

*"Remove it,"* she said.

His smile didn't move. *"Come now..."*

She turned her hand over, took hold of two of his fingers, and applied pressure. Approximately twelve percent of what she had used on the bandit in the clearing. Enough to communicate clearly. Enough to suggest the existence of considerably more.

Wei Longfeng made a small involuntary sound and went still.

*"I have questions,"* she said. Her tone was pleasant. *"You will answer them and then you will leave. Do you understand?"*

He nodded, carefully, trying not to move the fingers she was holding.

*"The martial artists. The cultivators. The Sects."* She released his hand. He pulled it back and held it against his chest. *"Tell me what you know."*

---

He told her what he knew. His voice was uneven for the first several sentences, and he checked his fingers twice before settling into something closer to his natural speaking rhythm.

His knowledge of cultivation theory was the knowledge of a man who had grown up adjacent to power without having any himself. Hearsay and impression rather than analysis. But impression had value. The Sects, as he described them, were vast and impersonal. They kept territory. They managed monster populations in the wilder regions. They occasionally went to war with each other in ways that affected the landscape. They did not, as a rule, concern themselves with the concerns of ordinary people.

*"They're recruiting?"* she said, when he mentioned it. *"The Grand Disciple Recruitment."*

*"Next month."* He had recovered some composure, though both hands remained in his own lap. *"At the Vermillion Capital. Every Sect sends representatives. Talents from all over the empire come to be tested."* Something shifted in his face, a brief tightening around the eyes. *"My father sent me three cycles ago. I was rejected at the first gate. Insufficient qi root, they said."*

He said this with the tone of someone repeating a wound they had not finished examining.

*"And the road to the Vermillion Capital,"* she said. *"The monster activity."*

He looked at her. *"You heard about that."*

*"I heard enough. Tell me more."*

He leaned forward. The performance of the first five minutes was gone now. What replaced it was something less polished and more genuine.

*"It's not normal activity,"* he said. *"Monsters have always come down from the high passes in lean seasons. Wolf-packs, the occasional stone bear, river serpents in flood years. This is different. Three caravans in six weeks. The patrol that came back..."* He paused. *"I know one of those soldiers. He's a drinking companion of my eldest brother. He won't discuss it. Not at all. Won't say what they saw."* He lowered his voice, not from dramatic effect but from the reflex of someone approaching a subject that unsettled him. *"There are rumors that it has something to do with a sect conflict. That something was disturbed in the deep mountains. Something old."*

*"How old?"*

*"The kind of old,"* Wei Longfeng said, *"that the Sects themselves speak of quietly."*

She processed this. A thousand li to the northeast. One month. Increased monster activity in the passes. Something of significant power disturbed in the mountain territories.

*"Thank you,"* she said, and stood.

He stared up at her. *"That's... you're leaving?"*

*"Our transaction is complete."*

*"I..."* He opened his mouth, closed it, opened it again. He was searching for a response and not finding one. *"I didn't even get your name."*

She looked down at the blue silk robe. The white blossoms at the hem. She had no name. The place where her designation should have been stored returned the same error message it had returned every time she had checked since the crater.

She needed something to give when asked. Something functional. Something that would do.

She looked at the blossoms again. White flowers on dark silk, stitched by someone she would never know, taken from a dead woman's luggage in a forest in a world she had fallen into by accident.

*"Baihua,"* she said. White Blossom.

She walked toward the door.

Behind her, Wei Longfeng sat at the table with his wine and his bruised fingers. He watched her go. Then he looked at the hand she had held, flexed his fingers experimentally, and raised his cup toward the server.

*"Another,"* he said.

---

She walked out of the Silver Wind Pavilion and stood on the street.

The sun was low. The market district was beginning its transition from afternoon commerce to evening activity. Lanterns were being lit in the shop fronts. The smell of cooking was stronger now, coming from a dozen directions simultaneously. A group of children ran past her, chasing a wooden hoop with sticks, their voices high and fast.

She had a direction. The Vermillion Capital. One month's travel. The Grand Disciple Recruitment, with every significant power in the empire gathered in one place, ostensibly for the business of recruiting new students, actually (she assessed, based on everything she had read and heard) for the more complex business of watching each other.

She needed to understand the threat landscape of this world before the threat landscape understood her. The Recruitment was the single best concentration of information available.

She turned north and began walking back toward her lodging (a sheltered alcove behind the grain storage buildings near the eastern wall, which she had identified on her initial survey as unused, dry, and out of the normal foot traffic pattern). She needed to plan the route. She needed to assess her resources. She needed to determine whether the monster activity in the passes between here and the Capital represented a serious threat to her transit or a manageable obstacle.

The core in her chest turned through its reactions, steady, continuous, generating heat at the center of her body. She could feel it against the inside of the alloy plating with each step.

She reached the alcove. She sat down with her back against the stone wall. She closed her eyes (she did not need to close her eyes, but closing them freed up optical processing capacity for internal work) and began planning.
